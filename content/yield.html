Total: $872 + $130 = $1,002, a gain of +0.2% in 2.5 years. The same $1,000 in a plain S&P 500 index fund, with dividends reinvested, became $1,550.

What happened

  1. The fund advertised a very high distribution rate: payouts in the last 12 months were about 98% of its price.
  2. Each payout came out of the fund. As the payouts left, the fund's price fell, from $193.50 to $25.29 (split-adjusted), −87%.
  3. Add the payouts back and Priya got roughly her own money back. The "income" was mostly her capital, returned in small pieces.

Why covered-call funds shrink

A covered-call fund owns shares and sells call options on them. The option premium pays for the distributions. The trade-off:

  • Upside is capped. When the shares jump, the option buyer takes the gain above the strike.
  • Downside is not. When the shares fall, the fund takes the whole loss.
  • Capped gains plus full losses, repeated over many weeks, wear the price down. If distributions exceed what the fund really earns, part of each payout is a return of capital: your own money, handed back.

Not every covered-call fund behaves this badly. Results depend on the strategy, the underlying holdings and the market path. The point is that the yield number alone cannot tell you.

The analogy

A tree gives fruit. Imagine picking the fruit and cutting off one branch every week. The basket looks fuller than your neighbour's, but the tree keeps getting smaller. Yield counts the basket. Total return measures the tree.

The Indian version

The same idea applies at home. Since April 2021, SEBI has required mutual funds to call the dividend option IDCW: Income Distribution cum Capital Withdrawal. The name says it: a payout can come from your own capital, and the NAV drops by the amount paid.

Five checks before any income product

  1. Total return, not yield. Compare price change plus all payouts against a simple index fund over the same dates.
  2. Price since launch. A steadily falling price with a high payout is the warning sign.
  3. What the payout is made of. US funds publish the split between income and return of capital (Section 19a notices). Indian funds show the NAV before and after IDCW.
  4. Costs. Expense ratio and trading costs come out every year, whatever the market does.
  5. Tax. Payouts may be taxed every year even when your investment shrinks. Check how your country taxes them.
