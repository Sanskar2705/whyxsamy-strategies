Did America beat India? The rupee did.

Two friends invested ₹1 lakh each in 2006. Rahul chose India's Sensex and Aman chose America's S&P 500. Twenty years later Aman had ₹5 lakh more. Most people read that as "America is better". The numbers say something else.

The story, step by step

  1. Rahul put ₹1,00,000 in the Sensex in June 2006. By June 2026 it was ₹7,20,868, or 7.2 times.
  2. Aman first changed ₹1,00,000 into dollars. In June 2006, $1 cost ₹45.81, so he got $2,183.
  3. In the S&P 500 his dollars grew to $12,888, or 5.9 times. In its own currency, the Indian market grew more (7.2× vs 5.9×).
  4. When Aman converted back in June 2026, $1 was worth ₹94.92. Every dollar brought home 2.07 times the rupees it cost.
  5. $12,888 × ₹94.92 = ₹12,23,347. The extra came from the exchange rate, not from American companies.

The formula

Return in ₹ = (1 + return in $) × (1 + change in ₹ per $) − 1
20 years: (5.904) × (94.92 / 45.81) − 1 = 12.23 − 1 → +1,123%

Per year: the S&P 500 made 9.28% in dollars and the rupee weakened about 3.7% a year, so in rupees it made 13.34% a year. Over the same period the Sensex made 10.38% a year in rupees.

What this means for you

If all your savings are in rupee assets (FDs, Indian stocks, Indian mutual funds, property), your wealth depends on one number: the rupee's value. When the rupee falls, anything priced in dollars (foreign education, travel, imported goods, a future in another country) costs more, and your rupee portfolio buys less of it.

This is currency risk. The textbook answer is currency diversification: hold part of your portfolio in assets earned in another currency. The aim is to reduce dependence on the rupee, not to chase a higher return. Over some periods the rupee strengthens and that part lags.

Ways Indian residents get foreign-currency exposure

  • International mutual funds / fund-of-funds sold by Indian AMCs: invested in rupees, holding foreign assets.
  • Direct foreign investing under RBI's Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS): up to USD 2,50,000 per financial year per person.
  • Check before you act: the tax on foreign assets, TCS on remittances, fund expense ratios, and the overseas-investment limits your fund house currently operates under. These rules change; read the latest from the fund house, your bank or a SEBI-registered adviser.

Questions to ask yourself

  • What share of my net worth is in rupee assets today? (Most people find it is close to 100%.)
  • Will I spend in dollars later: a child's education abroad, travel, emigration?
  • Am I comparing investments in the same currency? A US return quoted in rupees includes the exchange rate.
  • Can I hold a foreign allocation through years when the rupee rises and it lags?
